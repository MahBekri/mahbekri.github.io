Liquidity Delay Explorer

Purpose. A receivable can remain due in full without being available as cash when expected. This tool shows how a payment delay and an early withdrawal of funding move a bank's cumulative liquidity position over twelve months, and the first month-end at which it falls below the minimum buffer.

All amounts are in one illustrative unit, for example USD million.

Method. With delay share d and delay length k months, stressed inflow in month t is inflow(t) x (1 - d) + inflow(t - k) x d. With withdrawal share w, outflow in month 1 rises by w times the sum of outflows in months 2 to 12, and each later outflow falls by the same share. Position(t) is the opening buffer plus cumulative inflows less cumulative outflows. Extra buffer needed is the minimum buffer less the lowest stressed position, when that is positive.

Assumptions and limits. One currency and unit, monthly steps, and one delay applied to all inflows. The simulation assumes full eventual collection and does not model non-payment or recovery. Amounts deferred past month 12 fall outside the window. The withdrawal input is an illustrative acceleration of scheduled outflows, not a statement of what account holders may contractually withdraw. Positions are measured at month-end, so a shortfall inside a month is not shown. No management action, new funding or asset sale is modelled, and the buffer carries no haircut. The default figures are synthetic. This is a teaching demonstration: for scenario libraries, customer classes and haircuts see the ALM Stress Tester, and for context read the technical note.

Validation. Five test cases, with inputs and expected outputs, are published and were matched by a separate Python calculation. Entries that cannot be read stop the calculation. No independent human review has taken place. See the validation record.

Load a price or return series and get its statistics, a normality test, a stable-law fit, and value at risk and expected shortfall under historical, normal and stable models. Fitted results describe the sample and are not out-of-sample tests. Open in a full page

Purpose. Describes one return series and compares four ways of measuring its tail risk.

Method. Four kinds of result appear, and they should not be confused. Descriptive statistics and a Jarque-Bera test describe the sample. Fitted distributions are a normal law from the sample mean and standard deviation and a stable law fitted by McCulloch's quantile method, with stable expected shortfall integrated numerically. A conditional model, ARMA(1,1)-GARCH(1,1) with normal or stable innovations, lets value at risk move with volatility. A backtest counts breaches and applies the Kupiec, Christoffersen independence and conditional coverage tests.

Assumptions. The unconditional models treat returns as independent draws from one distribution. In the conditional model the stable tail index is kept between 1.05 and 2 so that expected shortfall stays finite.

Limits. Fitted figures and in-sample backtests describe the data they were fitted to. Only the out-of-sample option, which fits on the first 500 returns and refits every 250, tests a forecast. Passing a backtest on one series does not make a model right for another. Short series give unstable stable-law fits. Results are for one period and are not investment advice.

Validation. This is an earlier research tool that predates Sadid. It has not yet been benchmark-tested as part of the Sadid validation record.

Set the four parameters of a stable distribution and see its density, its distribution function and its tails against the normal. Open in a full page

Purpose. Shows how the shape and the tails of a stable law change with its four parameters, against the Gaussian case.

Method. The law is written in the S1 parameterisation, through its characteristic function: for a tail index a other than 1, exp(i m u - |s u|^a [1 - i b sign(u) tan(pi a / 2)]), with skewness b, scale s and location m. The density and distribution function are computed from Nolan's integral representation with Gauss-Legendre quadrature.

Assumptions and limits. At a = 2 the law is Gaussian with variance 2 s squared, so s is not a standard deviation. For a at or below 1 the mean does not exist and expected shortfall is infinite. For a below 2 the variance is infinite. Near a = 1 with non-zero skewness, the S1 form shifts the whole curve and it can leave the plotted range, and the calculation there is an approximation. Other texts use other parameterisations, so parameters are not interchangeable between sources without conversion.

Validation. The tool's own page reports agreement with SciPy to five significant digits for the density. That comparison has not been re-run as part of the Sadid validation record.
