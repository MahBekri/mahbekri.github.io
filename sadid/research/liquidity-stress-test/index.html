What a small Islamic bank needs from a liquidity stress test

Sadid Risk Analytics · published 19 November 2026 · version 1.0 · not peer reviewed

A liquidity stress test answers one question: if cash arrives later and funding leaves sooner than the contracts say, how long does the bank stay liquid? This note sets out the minimum a small institution needs to answer it, works one synthetic example, and states where the answer stops being reliable.

The decision question

The output a treasurer or an asset and liability committee needs is short: the number of weeks or months the bank survives under each scenario, the size of the shortfall where it does not, and which assumption drives the result. Everything else in the model exists to make those three figures defensible.

This is a different question from profit-rate sensitivity. A liquidity stress test measures whether cash is available when needed. A profit-rate test measures how earnings or economic value change when rates move. A bank can pass one and fail the other, and the two should not be read from the same table.

Four things the test needs

  1. A cash-flow ladder. Contractual inflows and outflows by time bucket, reconciled to the balance sheet. If the ladder does not reconcile, no scenario built on it means anything.
  2. Behavioural assumptions, written down. Contract terms set the scheduled cash flows and the options each party holds. Behavioural assumptions model how those rights are exercised and how often obligations are met late. For an Islamic bank the main ones are payment delays on Murabaha and Ijara receivables, early withdrawal from current and profit-sharing investment accounts, and the share of maturing wholesale funding that is renewed.
  3. A liquid buffer with a haircut. Cash and central bank balances count only to the extent they are unencumbered and can be used in a stress. Liquid Sukuk count after a haircut that reflects what they would fetch in the scenario, not on a normal day.
  4. A decision rule. A policy horizon the bank must survive, set by the board, so that each scenario ends in a clear status and not in a chart open to interpretation.

Why delay matters in a fixed-price sale

In a Murabaha the sale price is fixed when the contract is signed. In a pure delay, the model assumes the receivable remains due in full and is collected later, so the cash is not there on the contractual date and the bank must fund the gap. Restructuring, dispute, forgiveness and partial recovery are different cases and are not modelled. The model in this note treats that funding cost as carried by the bank. Whether any amount can be recovered for late payment depends on the contract, the institution's governing Sharia standard and the applicable law, so the assumption of no recovery is a deliberately cautious modelling choice, not a statement of what is permissible.

The model separates a temporary delay from an amount assumed never recovered: scheduled collection = collected on time + collected late + assumed unrecovered. This is a cash-flow distinction, not an accounting or regulatory definition of default, and a real default can still end in partial or full recovery. The example applies a non-payment rate first and a delay rate to what remains, and reports the two effects separately.

A worked example

The example is a synthetic small bank, in USD million. It holds 140 in cash and central bank balances and 260 in liquid Sukuk, and has 400 of undrawn financing commitments. The policy horizon is three buckets, so the bank must stay positive through 3-6M. Five of the workbook's six scenarios are discussed here. The sixth, defined by the user, is left out. The full ladder and every scenario rate are in the evidence files.

  • Payment delay: between 10% and 45% of each bucket's financing inflows, depending on the customer class, arrive one bucket late, and 1% is not paid.
  • Funding run-off: 10% of later current-account outflows, 20% of profit-sharing investment accounts and 8% of term deposits are withdrawn in the first month, and only 30% of maturing wholesale funding is renewed, against 80% in the base case.
  • Market shock: a 15% haircut on Sukuk with moderate delays and withdrawals.
  • Combined: heavier delays, heavier withdrawals, 20% rollover and an 18% haircut together.

What the example shows

Delay alone erodes the position without breaching. In the payment-delay scenario 844 of inflows arrive one bucket late. The lowest position falls from 233 to 160 and the bank survives all eight buckets. The funding cost of carrying the delay is 16.9, against 3.6 in the base case. That is an earnings cost, and it is reported apart from the liquidity position.

Funding behaviour breaches immediately. In the run-off scenario the position is already negative in the first month, at -57, and reaches -122 in the second bucket. The bank would need 122 of additional buffer to reach its horizon. The table below moves each run-off assumption from its base value on its own. In this model the effects add up exactly, and the largest is early withdrawal from profit-sharing investment accounts, followed by the lower rollover of wholesale funding.

The combined case is not the sum of the others. Its shortfall of 320 uses heavier parameters than the single-factor scenarios, so it cannot be read as delay plus run-off. It shows how far the position can fall when both happen with a market haircut. The bank recovers to a positive position only in the 1-2Y bucket.

What would change the answer

  • Where accounts with uncertain withdrawal dates are placed. Current accounts, and some investment accounts depending on their terms and notice periods, can be withdrawn at dates set by behaviour and not by a fixed maturity. Where the bank places them on the ladder can change the result materially and should be sensitivity-tested. The contractual terms of each account class must be reflected, and the placement documented and challenged.
  • The length of a delay. The model moves delayed cash by one bucket. One bucket is a month at the near end and years at the far end. A bank whose delays last several months needs a monthly ladder for the first year.
  • Management actions. No asset sales beyond the buffer, no new funding and no reduction in new financing are modelled. A real bank would act. The test shows the position before it does.
  • Calibration. Every rate in the example is illustrative. None is drawn from an institution, a market or a regulatory requirement.

Limits of this note

The example is one synthetic balance sheet under illustrative parameters. It shows how the calculation works and which assumptions matter. It is not evidence about any bank, and it does not show how often such scenarios occur. The model behind it is a prototype that has not been independently validated: its method and validation record are public. Displaced commercial risk, the pressure to pay investment account holders more than the assets earned, is not modelled. Liquidity, financing performance, funding spreads and profit rates interact in practice: reporting them separately does not make them independent.

What a risk team should verify next

  1. That the cash-flow ladder reconciles to the balance sheet at the reporting date.
  2. That each behavioural assumption has an owner, a source and a date.
  3. That the scenarios include one the bank fails, so that the size of the required buffer is known.
  4. That one scenario has been recalculated independently of the model.
  5. That the result is reported with its assumptions, not as a single number.

Standards for further reading

Two documents of the Islamic Financial Services Board are the natural starting points: IFSB-12, Guiding Principles on Liquidity Risk Management for Institutions offering Islamic Financial Services (March 2012), and IFSB-13, Guiding Principles on Stress Testing for Institutions offering Islamic Financial Services (March 2012). A bank's own supervisor sets the requirements that apply to it. This note names these documents as references. It does not claim that the model implements them.
