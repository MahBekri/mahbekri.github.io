Tail-Risk Screen

How far does a normal model understate, or overstate, the loss in the tail of this return series?

Prototype: the functions below exist and run on synthetic data. The workbook has not been independently validated and is not on sale. What the status labels mean.

Scope

What it does, and what it does not.

  • Computes value at risk and expected shortfall under a normal model, a Student-t model and the historical data, and value at risk under a Cornish-Fisher adjustment.
  • Compares like with like: expected shortfall against expected shortfall, value at risk against value at risk.
  • Reports skewness, excess kurtosis, a Jarque-Bera test, a Hill tail index and the count of losses beyond three volatilities.
  • Shows how many observations sit behind each historical tail figure, and the Hill index for five values of k.
  • Leaves every figure blank unless the data status is COMPLETE.
  • Accepts an optional second series, for example a screened portfolio beside its conventional parent.

It describes one sample. It does not forecast, it does not test models out of sample, and it does not show that screened portfolios have heavier tails than conventional ones. The normal model is not always the lower figure, and the workbook reports the cases where it is higher.

Inputs

What you enter.

Returns

Dates and periodic returns, up to 750 rows with no gaps. A comparison series is optional.

Parameters

Confidence level, minimum number of observations, the number of largest losses used by the Hill estimator, and an optional override of the Student-t degrees of freedom.

Method

How each figure is calculated.

Losses are per period and shown as positive numbers. With mean m, volatility s, tail probability a = 1 - confidence, and z the normal quantile at a:

Normal VaR = - (m + s x z) Normal ES = - m + s x pdf(z) / a

The Student-t model keeps the same mean and volatility. With v degrees of freedom and t the Student-t quantile at a:

scale = s x sqrt((v - 2) / v) t VaR = - (m + scale x t) t ES = - m + scale x pdf(t) / a x (v + t^2) / (v - 1) v = 4 + 6 / K, rounded, kept between 5 and 30 (K = excess kurtosis)

Historical VaR is the negative of the interpolated sample quantile. The historical tail average is the negative of the mean of returns at or below that quantile. It is a threshold-average approximation of expected shortfall: with few tail observations it is unstable and can differ from the formal empirical definition. The degrees of freedom above are a moment-matching heuristic, not a likelihood fit, and when excess kurtosis is zero or negative the workbook uses the upper bound of 30. Cornish-Fisher VaR adjusts z for sample skewness S and excess kurtosis K:

z_cf = z + (z^2 - 1) S / 6 + (z^3 - 3z) K / 24 - (2z^3 - 5z) S^2 / 36
H(k) = (1/k) x sum for i = 1 to k of ln( L(i) / L(k+1) ), Hill index = 1 / H(k)

Here L(1) to L(k+1) are the largest losses in descending order, and all must be positive. The estimate is meaningful only when the tail is close to a power law. Jarque-Bera is n/6 x (S^2 + K^2/4), compared with a chi-squared distribution with two degrees of freedom. It is an asymptotic test of normality in general, not a test of any tail model.

One worked case you can check.

The synthetic sample has 500 daily returns, analysed at 99% confidence. No real institution, portfolio or market is represented.

The historical tail average rests on five observations. Excess kurtosis is 0.95 and Jarque-Bera rejects normality. Four losses exceed three volatilities, where a normal model expects 500 x 0.00135 = 0.67 on one side. The Hill estimate of 3.7 is below 4, which the workbook raises as a diagnostic warning that the moment-based Student-t may be too light. Both estimates carry large sampling error on 500 observations, so this is a warning and not a finding.

Limitations

When not to rely on it.

  • The Student-t degrees of freedom come from a moment-matching heuristic, not maximum likelihood. Sample kurtosis is unstable in heavy-tailed data, so the fitted degrees of freedom are an indication.
  • The Student-t is symmetric. It does not capture a left tail heavier than the right. Stable and tempered stable models are not implemented in the workbook.
  • The Cornish-Fisher adjustment is unreliable for strongly skewed or very heavy-tailed samples.
  • Returns are treated as independent and identically distributed. Volatility clustering is ignored, so figures describe the whole sample, not current conditions.
  • The Hill estimate depends strongly on the number of losses used.
  • Historical figures at high confidence rest on few observations.
